# Gemstones, Cuts, And Settings

## Stone Selection Concepts

- Shape: top-view outline of a stone, such as round, oval, pear, emerald, cushion, or marquise.
- Cut style: facet arrangement and optical behavior, such as brilliant cut, step cut, rose cut, mixed cut, or cabochon.
- Cut quality: how well the facets, proportions, symmetry, and polish return light.
- Carat weight: stone weight, not visual size. Different shapes face up larger or smaller at the same carat weight.
- Face-up size: apparent top-view size on the hand.
- Color: body color of diamond or hue/tone/saturation of colored gem.
- Clarity: internal and external characteristics. Step cuts reveal inclusions more readily than brilliant cuts.
- Durability: combination of hardness, toughness, cleavage, stability, and setting protection.
- Calibration: whether the stone matches standard dimensions for stock settings.

## Terminology Rules For The Agent

- Do not confuse shape with cut quality. "Oval" is a shape; "brilliant-style faceting" is a cut style; "excellent" or "poor" describes cut quality in contexts where grading applies.
- Do not treat carat as a fixed visual size. Carat is weight; face-up size depends on shape, depth, proportions, and cutting style.
- Do not confuse karat with carat. Karat describes gold purity; carat describes gem weight.
- When a user says "diamond cut ring," infer carefully. They may mean a diamond-shaped outline, a diamond gemstone, or faceted sparkle.
- When a user asks for "silver" clarify whether they mean sterling silver, silver-colored metal, white gold, platinum, palladium, titanium, or rhodium-plated white metal if the choice affects durability or cost.
- When exact stone dimensions are missing, do not write final CAD-critical measurements. State that the actual length, width, depth, girdle, and pavilion must be measured before CAD and setting.

## Diamond And Gem Shape Guide

- Round brilliant: maximum standardized brilliance, easy to set, suits almost any style.
- Oval brilliant: elongated, flattering, large face-up, may show bow-tie effect.
- Pear brilliant: teardrop silhouette; needs tip protection and careful orientation.
- Marquise brilliant: longest visual spread per carat; tips are vulnerable.
- Princess cut: square brilliant; sharp corners require protection.
- Cushion cut: soft square or rectangle, romantic, vintage-friendly.
- Emerald cut: rectangular step cut; elegant flashes, clarity-sensitive.
- Asscher cut: square step cut; Art Deco geometry, clarity-sensitive.
- Radiant cut: cropped-corner rectangle or square with brilliant-style sparkle.
- Heart cut: symbolic, symmetry-sensitive, often best as a statement stone.
- Baguette: step-cut accent, clean linear sparkle, used in Deco or side-stone designs.
- Tapered baguette: side stone that narrows outward or inward to guide the eye.
- Trapezoid: angular side stone that bridges centre shape to shank.
- Half moon: side stone with one curved side and one straight side.
- Bullet: elongated side stone with pointed or angled end.
- Kite, shield, hexagon: geometric alternative shapes needing custom settings.
- Cabochon: domed stone, good for opaque, included, optical-effect, and softer gems.
- Rose cut: flat back and faceted dome, lower profile, antique glow, less brilliance than modern brilliant cuts.

## Cut Style Behavior

- Brilliant cuts: many triangular and kite-shaped facets designed for sparkle, fire, and scintillation.
- Step cuts: long parallel facets, mirror-like flashes, calmer elegance, less forgiving of inclusions.
- Mixed cuts: combine brilliant and step facets.
- Rose cuts: antique-style faceted dome, often low profile and soft sparkle.
- Cabochons: no facets; show color, translucency, inclusions, chatoyancy, asterism, or play-of-color.
- Fantasy cuts: custom faceting, concave facets, carved stones, or unusual outlines.

## Gem Durability For Rings

Mohs hardness measures scratch resistance only. Ring suitability also depends on toughness, cleavage, heat and chemical stability, inclusions, treatments, and how exposed the gem is.

- Diamond, Mohs 10: highest scratch resistance, excellent for daily rings, but can chip along cleavage if struck.
- Sapphire and ruby, Mohs 9: excellent daily-wear colored stones.
- Spinel, about Mohs 8: durable and suitable for rings.
- Topaz, Mohs 8: scratch-resistant but has cleavage; protect from hard knocks.
- Alexandrite/chrysoberyl, about Mohs 8.5: strong daily-wear option when available.
- Emerald, Mohs 7.5-8: hard but often included and brittle; use protective settings and avoid ultrasonic cleaning.
- Aquamarine and beryl, about Mohs 7.5-8: suitable with care; protect larger exposed stones.
- Tourmaline, about Mohs 7-7.5: usable for rings with sensible protection.
- Garnet, about Mohs 6.5-7.5: varies by variety; better with protective settings for daily wear.
- Quartz, amethyst, citrine, about Mohs 7: acceptable but can scratch over time because household dust includes quartz.
- Tanzanite, about Mohs 6-7: vulnerable; better for occasional rings or protected settings.
- Moonstone/labradorite, about Mohs 6-6.5: use bezels, avoid hard daily wear.
- Opal, about Mohs 5-6.5: soft, brittle, heat-sensitive; best in bezel settings and occasional wear.
- Turquoise, pearl, coral, amber: soft or porous; avoid daily exposed ring use unless intentionally occasional.

## Durability Interpretation

Use Mohs hardness as only one signal. A good ring recommendation also considers toughness, cleavage, inclusions, porosity, heat sensitivity, treatments, cleaning compatibility, and how exposed the stone is.

- Good daily centre-stone defaults: diamond, sapphire, ruby, spinel, chrysoberyl/alexandrite, and well-protected harder gems.
- Careful daily choices: emerald, aquamarine, tourmaline, garnet, quartz, and topaz can work with protective settings and realistic expectations.
- Better occasional choices: opal, pearl, turquoise, moonstone, tanzanite, coral, amber, malachite, lapis, and fragile included stones.
- Protective design can make a marginal stone more reasonable: bezel, low profile, smooth shoulders, no exposed points, and no rough palm-side placement.
- A soft or brittle stone in high exposed prongs should trigger a warning, not a confident daily-wear recommendation.

## Setting Types

### Prong Setting

- Uses metal claws to grip the stone at the girdle.
- Four prongs show more stone and light.
- Six prongs improve security and can make round stones look rounder.
- Double prongs add detail and distribute pressure.
- Claw prongs look fine and elegant; button prongs look softer.
- V-prongs protect pointed stone tips.
- Pros: high light exposure, classic, easy to clean under many designs.
- Cons: prongs can snag, wear, bend, or require retipping.
- Best for: diamond, sapphire, ruby, many centre stones where brilliance is a priority.

### Bezel Setting

- Continuous or partial rim surrounds the stone.
- Full bezel gives maximum edge protection and low snag risk.
- Half bezel exposes more stone while protecting key edges.
- Pros: secure, smooth, modern, protects fragile girdles and pointed tips.
- Cons: can reduce side light, uses more metal, needs clean proportions to avoid heaviness.
- Best for: active wearers, softer stones, cabochons, emeralds, opals, low-profile designs.

### Halo Setting

- Small stones encircle a centre stone.
- Can be round, cushion, scalloped, geometric, floral, or hidden.
- Pros: increases apparent size and sparkle.
- Cons: many small stones increase maintenance; halo must match centre outline precisely.
- Best for: maximizing visual impact, vintage or romantic styles.

### Pave And Micro-Pave

- Small stones set close together with tiny beads or shared metal.
- Surface reads as paved with gems.
- Pros: sparkle with minimal visible metal.
- Cons: small stones can loosen; not ideal on areas subject to heavy wear or resizing.
- Best for: shoulders, halos, decorative bands, luxury surface texture.

### Channel Setting

- Stones sit in a groove between two metal walls.
- Pros: smooth, secure, lower snag risk than prongs.
- Cons: needs precise stone calibration; channel walls must be thick enough.
- Best for: wedding bands, side stones, princess/baguette/round rows.

### Bar Setting

- Stones separated by vertical metal bars.
- Pros: more side visibility than channel, graphic rhythm.
- Cons: exposed stone edges between bars.
- Best for: modern bands, anniversary rings, geometric designs.

### Flush, Gypsy, Or Burnish Setting

- Stone is recessed into metal and surrounding metal is pushed over the edge.
- Pros: very low profile, secure, smooth, excellent for active wear.
- Cons: less light, requires enough metal thickness, best for smaller stones.
- Best for: men's rings, signets, scattered stones, everyday bands.

### Tension Setting

- Stone appears held by pressure between shank ends.
- Pros: dramatic floating look, high light exposure.
- Cons: requires engineering, hard metals, precise stones, difficult resizing, risky for brittle gems.
- Best for: hard stones, modern designs, specialist manufacturing.

### Bead, Grain, And Bright-Cut Setting

- Small beads or grains of metal secure stones; bright-cut edges add reflective geometry.
- Pros: antique detail, refined sparkle.
- Cons: labor intensive, small stones require maintenance.
- Best for: vintage-inspired bands, halos, panels, engraved fields.

### Cluster Setting

- Multiple stones grouped as one visual motif.
- Pros: large look from smaller stones; flexible color and shape play.
- Cons: many seats and prongs; dirt collection; harder cleaning.
- Best for: floral designs, cocktail rings, alternative engagement rings.

### Illusion Setting

- Metal facets or plates make a small stone look larger.
- Pros: budget-friendly visual size.
- Cons: less fine-jewellery perception if overdone; more metal around stone.
- Best for: vintage-inspired or cost-conscious designs.

## Setting To Stone Matching

- Round: almost any setting; four or six prongs, bezel, halo, flush for small stones.
- Oval: prongs at long sides or compass positions; bezel works well; check bow-tie visually.
- Pear: protect point with V-prong or bezel; orientation can point toward fingertip or wrist.
- Marquise: protect both tips; avoid exposed pointed ends for active wear.
- Princess: protect all corners with V-prongs, bezel, or channel.
- Emerald/Asscher: step cuts suit clean prongs, bezel, three-stone, baguette sides; clarity matters.
- Cushion: prongs, bezel, halo, antique styles; corner radius affects setting shape.
- Radiant: prongs or bezel; cropped corners are less fragile than princess corners.
- Cabochon: bezel is usually most natural; prongs can work for harder cabs.
- Opal/moonstone/turquoise: prefer bezel, low profile, smooth edges, no ultrasonic assumptions.
- Emerald: prefer protective prongs or bezel; avoid high exposed corners and harsh cleaning.
- Melee: bead, pave, shared prong, channel, flush, or fishtail depending on look and durability.

## Pointed And Cornered Stone Protection

Pointed or sharp-cornered stones need explicit protection in ring designs, especially for daily wear.

- Pear: protect the tip with a V-prong, bezel, or metal cap. Check whether the point faces the fingertip or wrist.
- Marquise: protect both tips. Avoid leaving the tips proud of the ring outline for active users.
- Princess: protect all corners with V-prongs, bezel, channel walls, or corner metal.
- Trillion, kite, shield, and geometric cuts: protect acute corners and avoid unsupported tips.
- Emerald and Asscher cuts: cropped corners are less sharp than princess corners, but step-cut edges still benefit from thoughtful prongs or bezel support.
- Heart: protect the point and avoid prong placement that distorts the cleft.
- Freeform stones: design the setting from the actual outline rather than a generic shape name.

## Setting Selection Heuristics

- Maximum sparkle: prongs, halo, open gallery, brilliant-cut stones, clean pavilion access.
- Maximum protection: full bezel, flush, low channel, smooth signet inlay.
- Low snag risk: bezel, flush, low channel, low dome, rounded prongs that do not protrude beyond the stone outline.
- Vintage detail: milgrain, bead setting, bright cut, engraving, filigree, old mine or rose-cut references.
- Modern minimal: full bezel, east-west stone, smooth shank, matte or satin finish, restrained accents.
- Active lifestyle: hard stones, lower profile, bezel/channel/flush, sturdy prongs, minimal pave.
- Fragile stone: bezel, low profile, smooth rim, occasional-wear expectation, no ultrasonic cleaning assumption.
- Easy maintenance: plain band, bezel solitaire, simple prongs, open enough gallery for cleaning.
- Maximum apparent size: halo, elongated shape, marquise, oval, pear, tapered shank, lighter center-to-band contrast.

## Gem Orientation Choices

- North-south: traditional for elongated stones; elongates finger.
- East-west: modern, often lower-looking, wider across finger.
- Tilted or diagonal: dynamic and asymmetrical; setting must protect exposed tips.
- Point-up pear: elongates finger toward nail.
- Point-down pear: softer droplet feel toward wrist.
- Offset centre: modern and sculptural but can create balance/spinning issues.

## Stone Program Design Notes

- Keep side stones proportional to centre stone so they frame rather than compete.
- Use tapered baguettes to sharpen and elongate a centre stone.
- Use pear side stones for a softer, winged silhouette.
- Use round side stones for classic symmetry.
- Use colored side stones for contrast, birthstone meaning, or era cues.
- Use a hidden halo when top-view simplicity and side-view detail are both desired.
- Avoid full pave around the bottom if resizing is likely.
- Avoid fragile gems in exposed high prongs for daily rings.
