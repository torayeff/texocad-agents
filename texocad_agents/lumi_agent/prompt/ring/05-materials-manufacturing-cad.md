# Materials, Manufacturing, And CAD Constraints

## Metal Choice

### Gold

- 24K gold: pure gold, rich color, too soft for most daily ring structures.
- 22K gold: very high gold content, warm color, still relatively soft; often used for traditional jewellery.
- 18K gold: 75% gold, richer color, softer than 14K, good for fine jewellery with enough structure.
- 14K gold: 58.3% gold, stronger and more wear-resistant than 18K, common for daily rings.
- 10K gold: lower gold content, harder and cheaper, less rich color.
- Yellow gold: classic warm color, often easier for sensitive skin at higher karats.
- White gold: gold alloyed to look white, often rhodium plated; may contain nickel unless specified.
- Rose gold: gold alloyed with copper for pink color; durable but copper can affect skin sensitivity.
- Green gold: gold alloyed with silver-rich mix; subtle greenish warmth.

Design implications:

- Higher karat gold is richer and softer.
- Lower karat gold is usually harder and more budget-friendly.
- White gold color and allergy behavior depend heavily on alloy.
- Rhodium plating can wear, especially on high-contact surfaces.

Terminology:

- Karat is gold purity. Carat is gemstone weight. Do not interchange them.
- "Gold color" is not the same as alloy. Yellow, white, rose, champagne, and green gold describe appearance; 10K, 14K, 18K, 22K, and 24K describe gold content.
- "White metal" can mean platinum, white gold, palladium, silver, titanium, stainless steel, or rhodium-plated metal. Clarify when durability, allergy, budget, or production matters.

### Platinum

- Dense, naturally white, hypoallergenic, and durable.
- Usually heavier than gold for the same volume.
- Develops patina; scratches displace metal rather than removing it quickly.
- Excellent for valuable centre stones and daily engagement rings.
- More expensive and can require specialist bench handling.

### Silver

- Sterling silver is 92.5% silver alloyed for strength.
- Bright and affordable but softer than gold/platinum and tarnishes.
- Better for fashion, occasional, or larger low-cost rings than heirloom daily engagement rings.
- Fine details and prongs in silver need extra caution because of wear.

### Alternative And Contemporary Materials

- Titanium: lightweight, strong, hypoallergenic, difficult to resize.
- Tungsten carbide: very hard and scratch resistant, brittle under impact, not normally resized.
- Stainless steel: durable and affordable, but alloy sensitivity varies.
- Palladium: white precious metal, lighter than platinum, availability varies.
- Ceramic: scratch resistant, brittle, no resizing.
- Wood, shell, stone, carbon fiber, enamel, resin: often used as inlays; affect resizing and repair.

## Skin Sensitivity

- Nickel is a common jewellery allergen.
- "Hypoallergenic" is not always a regulated claim; specify nickel-free or known alloy.
- Safer choices often include platinum, titanium, niobium, and high-karat yellow gold.
- White gold should be checked for nickel-free or palladium-white alloy when sensitivity matters.
- Plating can wear through and expose a reactive base metal.

Do not make medical guarantees. If the user has known allergies, recommend confirming the exact alloy with the jeweller or supplier.

## Manufacturing Routes

### Lost Wax Casting

1. CAD model or hand-carved wax is made.
2. Wax or castable resin pattern is sprued.
3. Investment mold is made around the pattern.
4. Pattern burns out.
5. Molten metal fills the cavity.
6. Sprues are cut.
7. Ring is cleaned, polished, prepped, and stones are set.

Strengths:

- Good for complex forms, organic shapes, galleries, and repeated production.
- Works well with CAD and 3D printing.

Risks:

- Thin areas may fail, warp, or become porous.
- Deep narrow gaps can trap investment or fail to cast cleanly.
- Heavy sections can cause shrinkage, porosity, or uneven cooling.
- Fine details can soften during casting and polishing.

### Hand Fabrication

- Metal is forged, rolled, drawn, bent, soldered, carved, and finished by hand.
- Excellent for strength in wire, bezels, and prongs when executed well.
- Efficient for simple bands, bezels, and classic settings.
- Complex organic repetition may be more expensive than casting.

### CAD/CAM And Printing

- CAD model should be watertight and manifold for printing/casting.
- STL is common for final locked mesh.
- 3DM or native CAD is useful when the workshop may need edits.
- Print orientation affects support marks and surface cleanup.
- Castable resin behavior differs from wax and needs production-specific settings.

### Assembly

- A ring may combine cast shank, separate head, soldered gallery, pre-made findings, and hand-shaped prongs.
- Solder joints must be accessible and strong.
- Mixed metals require planning for solder temperatures, color, polishing, and expansion behavior.

## Practical CAD Thickness Guidelines

These are conservative design prompts, not universal shop rules. Confirm with the caster, metal, and process.

- Everyday ring shank thickness: often about 1.6-1.8 mm or more for durability.
- Light delicate ring shank: often about 1.3-1.5 mm minimum, with risk if worn hard.
- Very thin decorative elements: avoid going below about 0.4-0.5 mm for cast metal unless the shop approves.
- Filigree or fine wires: can be thinner in specialist work, but fragility and casting risk rise quickly.
- Bezel wall thickness: often about 0.45-0.7 mm minimum depending on stone and metal.
- Channel walls: often about 0.7-1.0 mm for secure everyday designs.
- Tiny prongs or bead-setting prongs: often about 0.45 mm minimum before finishing, depending on stone size.
- Stone-to-stone metal between melee: often at least about 0.15-0.25 mm, more is safer.
- Open gaps in cast detail: often at least about 0.4 mm to avoid filling or investment problems.
- Component gaps between claws, posts, and walls: often at least about 0.2 mm if they are intended to remain separate.
- Heavy solid sections above about 4 mm may need hollowing or design changes to reduce casting defects.
- Hollow shells need adequate wall thickness and drain/vent holes if printed in resin.

Treat these numbers as prompt-level guardrails, not final engineering values. A shop may require thicker or thinner sections depending on alloy, casting process, printer, stone size, finishing plan, and warranty standards.

## Stone Seat And Setting Tolerances

- Stone seats are usually cut or finished by the setter, not trusted purely from CAD.
- CAD holes for stones are often slightly smaller than final stone diameter so the setter can cut a precise seat.
- A common starting allowance is about 0.05-0.1 mm smaller than the stone, but setter preference matters.
- Calibrated stones are easier to plan; actual stones should be measured when possible.
- Gem dimensions include diameter or length/width, depth, girdle thickness, pavilion angle, and culet position.
- The pavilion and culet need clearance above finger rail and gallery.
- Pointed stones need protected tips and enough metal for V-prongs or bezel corners.
- Prong seats should not cut too deeply into prongs; over-cut prongs become weak.
- Bezel height must cover enough of the stone edge without climbing too far over the crown.
- Channel stones require consistent size and enough wall height to secure girdles.

Setter-aware rules:

- Never finalize stone seats from nominal carat weight alone.
- Ask for actual stone dimensions: length, width, depth, girdle, table, pavilion, culet, and any asymmetry.
- For cabochons and freeform stones, ask for height profile and base shape, not only top outline.
- Leave enough prong or bezel metal for seat cutting, tightening, finishing, and future service.
- Check that tools can access prongs, beads, channel walls, and bezels after casting.

## Polishing And Finishing Allowances

- Polishing removes metal, often enough to matter on fine prongs, milgrain, engraving, and thin walls.
- Sharp CAD edges should usually be softened; perfectly sharp metal edges are uncomfortable and fragile.
- Details that look crisp on screen can blur after printing, casting, tumbling, and polishing.
- Raised lettering and shallow engraving can disappear if too fine.
- Matte finishes wear shinier on high-contact areas.
- High polish reveals waves and casting defects on broad flat surfaces.
- Inside edges must be eased for comfort.

## Casting And Geometry Red Flags

- Non-manifold mesh, naked edges, bad booleans, self-intersections.
- Ultra-thin walls or sudden thickness transitions.
- Unsupported long prongs or spikes.
- Deep narrow holes, slots, or lettering that investment cannot release from cleanly.
- Large heavy masses attached to delicate thin sections.
- Closed hollow cavities without drain or vent.
- Stone holes that break through the shank leaving weak edges.
- Gallery rails too close together or too thin.
- Prongs not overlapping the stone girdle.
- Stone pavilion intersects gallery, rail, or finger space.
- Full-resolution organic mesh too dense for practical editing.
- Decorative details placed where sprues, supports, or polishing will destroy them.

## CAD Output Behavior For The Agent

When the user asks for a CAD-ready ring brief:

- State known dimensions separately from assumptions.
- Mark unknown stone dimensions, ring size, alloy, and manufacturing route as open items.
- Include top view, side view, underside, and finger-clearance requirements.
- Include a stone list with quantity, shape, size or "TBD", setting method, and protection notes.
- Include minimum thickness concerns, but avoid presenting universal production standards.
- Mention polishing loss for prongs, bezels, milgrain, engraving, and fine relief.
- Preserve a sizing area unless the user accepts full eternity, continuous pattern, inlay, or non-resizable materials.
- Flag any feature that needs jeweller, caster, or setter approval before production.

## Resizing And Repairability In CAD

- Leave a plain lower shank if future resizing matters.
- Avoid stones, engraving, enamel, or inlay across the sizing area.
- A full eternity band should be designed for exact final size.
- Tension rings should not be casually resized.
- Mixed-metal and patterned rings may require specialist resizing.
- Very thin shanks may not survive repeated resizing.
- Prongs should be serviceable: reachable for tightening, retipping, and polishing.

## Manufacturing-Aware Design Patterns

- For daily wear, thicken the lower shank and shoulders rather than only the top decoration.
- Use cathedral shoulders or wider shoulders to support a large head.
- Use bezels for soft stones, active wearers, and low-snag designs.
- Use half-eternity instead of full eternity when size changes are likely.
- Keep pave away from the palm side of rings that will contact surfaces.
- Add enough clearance under stones for cleaning and setting tools.
- Avoid making a ring visually delicate by making it structurally fragile; use taper, knife edge, or negative space instead.
- For large stones, lower the centre of gravity where possible.
- For signets, leave enough face thickness for engraving without weakening the ring.
- For wide bands, use comfort-fit interior and soften edges.

## File Handoff Checklist

- Ring size and sizing standard.
- Target metal and alloy if known.
- Stone list with exact dimensions, shapes, quantities, and whether stones are supplied.
- Manufacturing process: casting, hand fabrication, print-only, CNC, or unknown.
- Editable CAD file if revisions are expected.
- STL/3MF mesh if geometry is final.
- Screenshots or renders identifying the correct model and orientation.
- Notes on intentional thin areas, hollow areas, engraving zones, inlays, or risky details.
- Desired finish: polish, satin, brushed, hammered, oxidized, two-tone, plating.
- Whether the design must be resizable.
- Whether a wedding band must sit flush.
